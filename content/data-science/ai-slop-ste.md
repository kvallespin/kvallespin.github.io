# The cure for AI slop is a boring 40-year-old manual

<p><img src="assets/ai-slop-ste/ai-slop-ste-main-banner.png" alt="An aircraft maintenance manual beside a laptop checking AI-generated technical writing for clarity and consistency"></p>
<p class='caption'><em>Banning AI writing tells treats the symptoms. A checkable writing system fixes the structure.</em></p>

If you have worked with Large Language Models lately, you have probably tried the usual cure for bad AI writing: the ban list.

No em dashes. No “delve.” No “tapestry.” No “realm.” No “in today’s fast-paced world.”

That can help. But only at the surface.

A paragraph can avoid every banned word and still sound empty. It can pass the “no AI tells” checklist and still feel vague, inflated, and oddly frictionless. The problem is not vocabulary. The problem is structure.

---

## Slop has mechanics

AI slop has recognizable habits. The model changes terms for no reason. It hedges until the claim disappears. It turns verbs into nouns, writing “conduct an analysis” instead of “analyze.” It leans on adjectives like “robust,” “seamless,” and “dynamic” without giving evidence. It writes long sentences that bury the point.

These are mechanical habits. That matters. Mechanical habits can be named, checked, and constrained.

---

## Aviation had this problem first

Aviation solved a version of this problem decades ago through Simplified Technical English. The goal was not elegant prose. The goal was clarity under pressure. Maintenance instructions had to be clear enough for non-native English-speaking mechanics to understand without guessing.

<p><img src="assets/ai-slop-ste/asd-ste100-ai-white-paper-banner.png" alt="ASD-STE100 Simplified Technical English and Artificial Intelligence white paper banner"></p>
<p class='caption'><em><a href="https://www.asd-ste100.org/assets/files/WhitePaper-ASD-STE100_and_AI.pdf">ASD-STE100 Simplified Technical English and Artificial Intelligence (AI), STEMG white paper, June 2026</a></em></p>

The rules are blunt: use one term for one thing, prefer active voice, keep sentences short, avoid phrasal verbs, and use verbs for actions. Do not hide meaning inside official-sounding language.

---

## Ban lists are not systems

That discipline maps cleanly to AI writing. A blacklist tells the model what not to use. A writing system tells it how to write.

| Ban-list approach | Writing-system approach |
|---|---|
| Removes visible tells | Reduces structural habits |
| Says what not to use | Says how to write |
| Easy to bypass | Easier to check |
| Fixes surface style | Improves clarity at the sentence level |

This does not mean every piece of writing should sound like an aircraft manual. Voice still matters. Judgment still matters. A controlled rule set can make writing clearer, but it cannot make a weak idea strong or a false claim true.

---

## The practical line

Use strict rules for procedures, error messages, technical docs, pull request descriptions, and agent output. Use lighter rules when the writing needs personality. In both cases, check the output instead of trusting the prompt.

Stop playing whack-a-mole with banned words. Define the writing standard for the task. Make the output pass a test. Then verify the substance separately.

Clean writing is not the same as correct writing. But it gives the reader a fighting chance.
