# Revisiting Mynt: the cardboard appraisal

*A follow-up to my Mynt/GCash valuation.*

![Valuation ranges from the Mynt prospectus against the PHP 6.60 offer price. Two DCFs and a book-value identity cluster near PHP 2.00; the peer-multiple route is the only bar that reaches the PHP 10.00 ceiling set in July 2026, since superseded. The cerise marker is the 6.60 offer price of 2 October 2026, sitting above most of the model range. Source: author's analysis of the Mynt prospectus and offering range.](assets/mynt-cardboard/football_field.png)

> **Disclosure:** I use AI as a writing partner to refine my prose and structure, but the ideas, analysis, and first drafts originate entirely with me.

Everyone knows a piece of Pokémon cardboard can sell for five figures. We don’t call those buyers delusional (us mere mortals might call them “insane” in every sense of the word). A rare card is scarce, and the story attached to it amounts to real money to whoever owns it. The appraiser pricing the cardboard isn’t wrong. He’s just answering a different question than the one the buyer is asking.

In July I valued Mynt, the company behind GCash, with three respectable methods off its own prospectus. Two DCFs and a book-value identity landed in the neighborhood of two pesos a share. 10,000 Monte Carlo runs spread wider and never cleared five. One stretchable route, peer earnings multiples, reached the promised PHP 10.00 ceiling, and I didn’t believe it (not even for a hot minute). So my conclusion was hedged to within an inch of its life: you don’t get from the prospectus to PHP 10.00 with the tools I was taught, and anyone who does is paying for something my model cannot see.

On October 2 they priced the shares at 6.60. Through September the company kept saying not to expect ten, floated a higher range that slid down through seven; then BlackRock and Fidelity anchored at 6.60 with demand reportedly several times the offering (!). The floor of my appraisal never moved. The market simply chose to pay above it (albeit constrained by a force called “reality”).

That gap between PHP 2.00 and 6.60 isn’t a disagreement. It’s the two questions again. I was asking what the thing is worth. They were asking what someone will pay, and that question has an intangible element to it I never modeled with Excel or wrote a line of Python for. 70% of the offering went to institutions, with billions in demand before retail got a window. That money wanted something scarce carrying a story, a profitable consumer franchise in a country of 120 million where half the adults already use the app every month. Again, we’re back to SCARCITY + STORY. It’s a distribution problem before it’s a valuation problem, and my model has no row for either (being the casual analyst, the B-school grad student that I am). My guess is my model answers a narrower question than the exchanges did. Narrower isn’t wrong. Sometimes narrow is just narrow.

The IPO is on October 20, so the rest is speculation. Lockups mean much of the float sits frozen while the rest turns over. Quiet weeks, prices that barely move on news. Then the expiry arrives and whoever the clearing price assumed would hold stops being them. As for my fearless forecast: within six months the stock trades closer to my Monte Carlo band than to 6.60, and I write a third post about it. Or it doesn’t, and the institutions were right about something my model was blind to.

Either way, the interesting signal was never anybody’s valuation. It was a room full of people with real information deciding the cardboard price was not the interesting number, and getting what they wanted.

The rest, really, is just left to your judgment.
